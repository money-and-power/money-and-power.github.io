When everyone went to bed on Tuesday night, they were expecting a good night for the democrats but they woke up to something much bigger. They didn’t just win the house, but they took the Senate too, and as of Thursday CBS News has them with at least 54 seats there, with Alaska and Mississippi still uncalled. In the house they’re on pace for around 256 seats, up from 214.

People around the world have been digesting this blue tsunami, some people think it’s good, others bad, and for me I keep landing in the same uncomfortable place. I think the result is a shock, and I think it was mostly a verdict on Donald Trump. I think it’s mostly good that voters got to deliver it, and I think a lot of what people are hoping it will do, protecting Medicaid and food assistance and health insurance, is the part the election did the least to actually fix.

## Why it's shocking

Midterms are almost always bad for the president’s party. That’s close to a law of American politics, so a loss for the Republican’s party by itself wasn’t a surprise. The actual surprise is where it happened and the extent.

Democrats flipped Texas, where James Talarico beat Ken Paxton despite Elon Musk reportedly spending over $150 million trying to stop it. They flipped Ohio, Iowa, and Kansas, three states Trump won easily in 2024. In South Carolina, counties Trump won swung left by 21 points on average (New York Times). Susan Collins lost in Maine. These are not places where Democrats had spent years building a lead. When states Trump won comfortably swing this hard in two years, the simplest explanation is that voters were responding to him and what his government has done. And Mississippi, where no Democrat has won a Senate race since 1982, was still too close to call on Thursday afternoon, with the Democrat ahead by only about 1,700 votes.

The House looked very similar. Republicans redrew maps in several states to protect their majority, and CBS reports they were on track to lose almost every district they tried to gerrymander. Richard Hudson, who ran the Republicans’ own House campaign committee, lost his seat. David Valadao, one of the ten House Republicans who voted to impeach Trump, lost too. That last one is worth noticing: it means that being willing to break with Trump once doesn’t protect a Republican from being pulled down with him.

What is very interesting is that the polls just didn’t see this coming. Hakeem Jeffries said as much, joking that Mike Johnson had been right when he said the polls were wrong. Johnson was right for the wrong reasons, but it’s still a good reminder to be careful with every confident prediction you’ve read, including the ones you agree with.

## What the voters were reacting to

What the new voters were reacting to isn’t a mystery. It was a vote against Trump, and the reasons were mostly about money. In the following I want to be careful about how much weight I put on for any one reason, because I don’t have the entire poll yet. What I do have points the same way though. A KFF poll in January found that among voters who said letting the ACA (Affordable Care Act) credits expire was a mistake, 80 percent blamed Trump or congressional Republicans most, and three-quarters of all voters said the price of health care would affect how they vote in the fall. In late September, an AP-NORC poll found that 65 percent of Americans blamed Trump’s policies more than outside factors for persistently high prices. For a lot of people this wasn’t a vote for a Democratic spending agenda, but it was a way to put a check on Trump, and Medicaid and premiums were the reasons it felt personal.

A Reuters/Ipsos poll in late September had Trump’s overall approval at 32 percent, the lowest of his political career, and the AP-NORC poll had only 17 percent of Americans approving how he’s handling the cost of living. Reuters/Ipsos reported that respondents named the cost of living as the top factor in how they’d vote, so a number that low on that issue was always going to show up on election night.

Trump’s own decisions are a big part of why, and he essentially buried himself. Here are a few of the main reasons:

**Tariffs.** The Supreme Court ruled 6-3 in February that the “Liberation Day” tariffs were illegal. Even after that, Yale’s Budget Lab projected the average US tariff rate would settle around 9 percent once the replacement tariff expired, which would still be the highest since 1946 outside of 2025, and estimated that the tariffs in place cost the typical household somewhere around $600 to $800 a year. The administration answered the ruling with a temporary tariff under a different law, which was limited to 150 days and expired in July. Meanwhile, the government has to refund most of the roughly $166 billion collected under the old tariffs. Those go to the businesses that paid them, not to the families who paid higher prices at the register.

**The Iran War.** The US joined Israel in strikes on Iran on February 28. By May the Pentagon told Congress the war had cost $29 billion, a number Senator Patty Murray called “suspiciously low,” and which didn’t include damage to US bases. Gas prices hit a four-year high in mid May, and the latest reading put inflation at 3.8 percent. Efforts in Congress to limit the war failed more than once. I’m not going to relitigate whether the war was justified, but a war nobody voted to fund, paid for with borrowed money, is a spending decision like any other.

**Health care.** The enhanced ACA subsidies expired at the end of 2025. KFF projected last fall that people getting tax credits on the marketplace would see their premium payments roughly double, from about $888 to $1,904 a year on average. The measured increase turned out smaller, 58 percent across all enrollees, because many people switched to cheaper plans. But over a million fewer people signed up for 2026, and by February, paid enrollment had dropped from 21.8 million to 19.2 million, according to KFF’s analysis of federal data.

Add the Medicaid and SNAP cuts in last year’s reconciliation law and you get a government that, in one year, made health insurance and groceries more expensive for some of the people with the least room to absorb it.

## Why it's good that Democrats have more control

My argument isn’t that one party is better than the other. It’s that the last two years showed what happens when nobody on the inside can say no to a president, whichever party he belongs to.

The 2025 reconciliation law is the clearest example. CBO estimated it would add about $3.4 trillion to the deficit over ten years while leaving around 10 million more people uninsured. I can imagine someone defending that trade, but I can’t imagine anyone saying it was seriously tested. The tariffs and the war are the same story: it took the Supreme Court, not Congress, to stop the biggest tariffs, and efforts in Congress to limit the Iran War failed more than once.

Divided government is a slow, irritating answer to that, and it’s mostly the right one. Hearings, subpoenas, and the power to block a budget make both sides defend their numbers to someone who isn’t on their team. Representative Robert Garcia, the incoming Oversight chair, has already named the administration’s business dealings, DHS and ICE, and the Epstein files as targets, and Chuck Schumer has promised to “entangle” Trump in “legislative razor wire” if he defies the law. Whatever you think of those targets, I’d want someone able to ask no matter who was in the White House.

## Why it's bad for social spending

Here’s the point where I stop agreeing with the sentiment of the week, which is mostly relief that Trump now has someone standing in the way.

Start with what the Democrats say they’ll do. Jeffries’ first priorities are reversing Medicaid and SNAP cuts, extending the expired ACA credits, ending the Iran war, and rolling back tariffs. Put a price on the list. The Medicaid and SNAP cuts in the 2025 law came to roughly $1.1 trillion over ten years. CBO estimated in September 2025 that a permanent extension of the ACA credits would cost about $350 billion over ten years. That’s around $1.5 trillion for two items on the list, and nobody has said where it comes from. Ending the Iran war should save money, so that item helps. But the fourth item, tariffs, is where I think the list runs into trouble.

Tariffs are just taxes, and the government has been counting on that money. After the Supreme Court ruling, CBO’s latest projection has the 2026 deficit at about $2.1 trillion, roughly $200 billion above its February estimate, mostly because tariff collections came in around $250 billion lower than expected. CBO estimated the ruling would cut revenue by about $1.6 trillion through 2036, and with higher interest costs, add about $2 trillion to deficits over that period. So the thing that raised prices at the register has also raised money for the government, and every tariff Democrats roll back widens a gap that was already bigger than the one in Figure 3 below. I think rolling them back could be the right call, but we have to wait and see.

Restoring these programs costs money, and the country is already in a deep hole. CBO’s February projections have the 2026 deficit at about 5.8 percent of GDP, rising to 6.7 percent by 2036. Debt held by the public is about 101 percent of GDP and heading to 120 percent, which would pass the record set right after World War II. That’s the situation that Democrats are inheriting, and they have to be able to deal with it.

Interest is the part that squeezes everything else. CBO projects net interest going from a little over $1 trillion this year to more than $2.1 trillion in 2036, or from 3.3 percent of GDP to 4.6 percent. That money buys no health care, no food assistance, and no schools. Every restoration paid for with borrowing makes that line a little bigger, and unlike a program, nobody can vote to cut it.

I’m not saying that there’s no way to pay for it. The same 2025 law that cut Medicaid also cut taxes by several trillion dollars, and the most direct way to fund a restoration is to reverse some of those tax cuts. That’s the real fight, and it’s also the one Trump would veto. So I expect any restoration to go through on credit or not at all, and I don’t think the party that just won has decided which.

Here’s the thing that I think gets lost. Because so much of this vote was against Trump, it’s not clear Democrats got a mandate for any specific spending plan. They got a mandate to push back, which is easier to deliver than a budget. A party can be united by opposition to a president and still split the day it has to decide what to cut and what to tax. I can’t prove that will happen, but it’s the usual way these coalitions break, and the programs Democrats want to protect are the ones most exposed if it does. If interest costs keep growing faster than the economy, Congress will eventually be forced to choose, and it’s usually the programs that serve people with the least political power that get cut first. The people who benefit from restoring Medicaid are also the people with the most to lose if it’s restored on credit and later clawed back in a crisis.

Timing makes it worse, because the damage starts before the new Congress can do anything about it. The new Congress takes office in January. Medicaid work requirements are set to begin on January 1, 2027, with the federal funding changes and copays following in 2028. CBO estimated the work requirements alone would cost nearly 5 million people their coverage. Undoing that takes a bill Trump signs, and he has no reason to sign one without getting something in return. Meanwhile, states have to balance their budgets. A RAND analysis found that state Medicaid budgets are expected to shrink by about $664 billion over the decade, and CBO assumes states will replace only about half of the lost provider-tax revenue. That means cuts to services and hospitals in a lot of states whether or not Congress is fighting about it, and rural hospitals are the ones most often named at risk.

The calendar makes it worse still. The 2026 Trustees reports put the Social Security retirement fund running out in late 2032, which would mean an automatic cut of around 22 percent, and Medicare’s hospital fund in 2033.

Fixing that takes a deal between both parties that raises revenue, trims benefits, or does some of each. A Democratic Congress facing Trump and his veto pen doesn’t look like the setup for that deal. Trump has little reason to hand Democrats a win, and Democrats have a lot of political reason to fight him instead of cut a deal with him. Both incentives point away from the compromise the trust funds need. The more likely outcome is that each side defends its position, nothing passes, and the deadline keeps getting closer while both sides say they protected the program.

There’s also a limit on what the win delivers. Even at 54 seats, Democrats are short of the 60 votes that most major legislation needs, and Trump’s veto would stop anything that did pass. So the tools they have left are rather blunt: shutdowns, debt-limit standoffs, and spending riders. Those fights hurt the most vulnerable first, because they’re the ones who need the benefit checks to arrive on time. Winning the Senate raises the stakes of those fights, but it doesn’t end them.

None of this means the cuts were right or that benefits shouldn’t be defended. I think the cuts were a mixed idea, with stopping the cuts being beneficial but deciding who pays what’s left and how is the hard part.

## What I'm not saying, and what would change my mind

I’m not saying Democrats caused the deficit, because they didn’t, any more than Republicans alone did. I’m not saying every problem on the list is Trump’s fault either, and many of these negatives such as the trust fund dates and interest bill preceded him.

What we need to see as voters is what the Democrats do next. We need to see a plan that restores spending with real revenue or offsets, and that CBO scores would show us they’re treating money as real. A bipartisan Social Security fix moving through committee before the next election would show a divided government can still do hard things. And if Medicaid and SNAP get restored without interest costs becoming a bigger problem (good luck with that), then I was too pessimistic about the math.

Until then, I think the right reaction to this week is to hold two things together. Voters were right to rebuke the president whose decisions made their lives more expensive, and it’s good that someone can finally say no to him. It’s also true that saying no to Trump, or to a cut, is not the same as paying for everything that’s left. The main takeaway is that there’s people behind the parties, but the ideas are what unite and divide.
